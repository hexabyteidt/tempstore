# Verification log: B5 — `thurston_tarcreek_hemp_selenium`

## Decision

**Final status: EXCLUDED.** The candidate record is bibliographically misidentified: the cited title is not a Thurston/Tar Creek study. It is Stonehouse et al. (2020), an Environmental Science & Technology paper with DOI **10.1021/acs.est.9b07747**, plus the author’s 2019 Colorado State University thesis. The primary source was retrieved and inspected. It contains (i) a naturally seleniferous field survey in Colorado and (ii) separate greenhouse experiments in Turface with deliberately supplied sodium selenate. The field survey is relevant to species and natural contamination, but no directly compatible soil Se concentration for the sampled plots is reported, no BCF formula or BCF values are reported, and the published field plant concentrations therefore cannot be used to calculate a compatible BCF. The greenhouse arm is ineligible under the locked protocol because it is an artificial selenate dose in a gravel medium/nutrient-solution system. Consequently, there are no eligible, primary-source-verified BCF observations.

## Species/taxon

The source explicitly studies hemp, **Cannabis sativa L.** The thesis Chapter 2.2.2 identifies the experimental material as *C. sativa* var. “Workhorse” (thesis PDF p. 28); the publication title and PubMed record also state *Cannabis sativa* L. This matches the locked target species. No taxonomic ambiguity was found.

## Contamination origin and experimental design

### Naturally contaminated field survey

The thesis Chapter 2.2.1 “Field surveys” (PDF p. 28) states that hemp seed, flower, and leaf samples were collected in September–October 2017 from four Colorado agricultural areas: Colorado State University Agriculture Research Development and Education Center (ARDEC) in Fort Collins; two commercial hemp fields in Eaton and Otis farmed by Colorado Cultivars Co.; and Arkansas Valley Research Center in Rocky Ford. Samples were dried at 50 °C for three days and acid-digested for elemental analysis. The thesis Chapter 2.3.1 “Field Survey” (PDF pp. 32–33) states that plants were grown on “naturally seleniferous soils” at the four locations. The publication abstract independently describes field surveys in naturally seleniferous agricultural areas in Colorado.

The thesis uses USGS county-level soil Se information only as contextual comparison: reported county values are Otis 0.29 ± 0.15 (range 0.10–1.11), ARDEC 0.55 ± 0.35 (0.11–2.55), Eaton 0.58 ± 0.42 (0.10–3.18), and Rocky Ford 1.30 ± 0.56 (0.46–3.47). These values are reported in Chapter 2.3.1 (PDF p. 32) as corresponding-county USGS data, not as directly measured soil concentrations from the individual sampled plots. Figure 2.1A is a USGS soil-Se map and inset; Figure 2.1B plots plant-organ Se concentrations from the four sampling areas. The Figure 2.1 caption (PDF p. 33) states that plant values are means and standard errors, n=3–9, and that not all organs were available from every site.

The source therefore verifies **field-derived, naturally contaminated exposure**, but it does not supply a compatible plot-level soil denominator paired with each plant observation. No amendment, biochar, chelator, dilution, or experimental Se spike is described for this field-survey arm.

### Controlled greenhouse experiment

The thesis Chapter 2.2.2 “Seedling experiments” (PDF pp. 28–29) states that 30 seedlings were transplanted into 4 × 4 cm pots containing Turface gravel growth medium. Treatments were 0, 40, 80, 160, and 320 µM Se applied as Na2SeO4 in one-fifth-strength Hoagland nutrient solution; plants were harvested after 25 days. The thesis Chapter 2.2.3 “Mature plant experiments” (PDF pp. 29–30) states that 72 plants were transplanted into 2-L pots filled with Turface, supplied weekly with half-strength Hoagland solution, and, after three weeks, treated once weekly for 12 weeks with 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, or 80 µM Na2SeO4 (n=12 per treatment), for a total of 15 weeks. The paper abstract gives the same design in condensed form: seedlings in Turface supplied with 40–320 µM selenate and mature plants in Turface supplied with 5–80 µM selenate.

This greenhouse arm is **not eligible**: it is artificial sodium-selenate dosing in Turface/nutrient solution rather than naturally contaminated or field-derived soil. It is also not a soil BCF experiment.

## Plant and soil concentrations; BCF verification

The source reports plant Se concentrations from the field survey and greenhouse experiments, but it does not report a compatible BCF. Relevant source locations are:

* Thesis Chapter 2.3.1 “Field Survey,” PDF pp. 32–33: field seed concentrations average approximately 15–25 mg Se/kg dry weight; flowers and leaves approximately 5–10 mg Se/kg dry weight; Figure 2.1B shows site/organ means ± SEM, n=3–9.
* Thesis Chapter 2.2.7 “Elemental analysis,” PDF p. 31: root, stem, leaf, flower, and seed samples were dried and analyzed by ICP-OES after acid digestion; the stated sample-material quantification limit is approximately 1–2 mg/kg DW.
* Thesis Chapter 2.3.2 and associated figures (PDF pp. 35 onward) and the journal abstract report greenhouse Se accumulation up to approximately 1,300–1,400 mg/kg shoot DW in seedlings and up to approximately 200 mg/kg DW in mature leaves, flowers, and seeds under selenate treatments. These are artificial-dose results and cannot be used for the locked dataset.
* Supporting Information Table S1, PDF p. 2, reports seed nutrient concentrations across 0–80 µM selenate treatments and gives n=12. It does not report a BCF, soil concentration, or field soil denominator. The remaining supplement pages do not provide a compatible field soil/plant BCF dataset.

A full-text search of the downloaded thesis and supporting information found no reported “BCF” or “bioconcentration factor” definition/formula. The source does not state plant/soil, shoot/soil, whole-plant/soil, or root/soil as a BCF numerator/denominator. The field plant values cannot be divided by the county-level contextual USGS values because those are not directly reported concentrations for the sampled plots and do not establish a compatible paired denominator. Per the protocol, values must remain missing rather than inferred.

## Overlap and independence audit

The supplied overlap audit lists seven confirmed DOI overlaps with the primary dataset; **10.1021/acs.est.9b07747 is not among them**, and the audit reports zero probable DOI overlap flags. The supplied candidate and priority files incorrectly place this Stonehouse selenium paper under a Thurston/Tar Creek candidate ID, apparently because the discovery pass associated “Thurston” with another hemp candidate. Direct primary-source inspection resolves the identity mismatch.

The actual Thurston Tar Creek paper is “Evaluation of Industrial Hemp Cultivar and Biochar Rate to Remediate Heavy-Metal-Contaminated Soil from the Tar Creek Superfund Site, USA,” *Soil Systems* 8(4), 114 (2024), DOI 10.3390/soilsystems8040114. Its publisher page states that BCF is calculated as aboveground tissue concentration divided by initial water-soluble soil heavy-metal concentration and that the experiment varies Tar Creek soil contamination level, hemp cultivar, and 0/2/5/10% biochar rates; the reported elements are Cd, Pb, and Zn. It is not the Stonehouse selenium paper and does not provide evidence that the Stonehouse Colorado samples and Thurston Tar Creek pots share experimental units. The candidate is therefore **not a duplicate/shared experiment with the Thurston Tar Creek study**, but its candidate ID/title attribution is erroneous.

## Why no observations are returned

The only field-derived portion satisfying the natural-contamination and species screen reports plant Se concentrations but lacks a directly reported compatible soil concentration for the sampled plots and lacks a BCF definition. The only soil-like denominator available in the source is contextual county-level USGS data, which cannot be substituted for paired sampled-soil concentrations. The greenhouse concentrations are from artificial selenate dosing in Turface/nutrient solution and are locked out regardless of their numerical detail. Accordingly, all observation-level fields remain absent and the observations array is empty.
