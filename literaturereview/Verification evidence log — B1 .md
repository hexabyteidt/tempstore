## Final determination

**Final status: `INCLUDED`.** The primary publication is verified and reports an eligible subset: naturally contaminated, field-derived Tar Creek soil used in a greenhouse pot experiment with *Cannabis sativa* L. industrial hemp. The study contains a biochar amendment factor (0, 2, 5, and 10% v/v), so the 2/5/10% treatments are not admitted to the locked unamended target set. The **0% biochar control arm** is unamended with respect to biochar and is eligible for extraction. Six exact Cd BCF observations (three soil contamination levels × two hemp cultivars) are directly reported in Table 5. Pb and Zn BCF values are discussed and plotted, but the exact 0%-biochar-by-cultivar values needed for treatment-specific extraction are not provided in a numeric table/text; they are therefore left out rather than digitized or inferred. The 2024 article is a publication from the same experimental campaign as Thurston’s 2023 University of Arkansas thesis, which is documented as a shared/duplicate publication for that thesis, but it is independent from Ha et al. 2025 and from the separate B2/B5 candidate studies.

## Exact species/taxa and target elements

The exact target is **industrial hemp, *Cannabis sativa* L.** The two named cultivars are **‘Carmagnola’ and ‘Jinma’** (MDPI article abstract and Sections 1, 2.2, 2.5). The measured elements are **cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and zinc (Zn)**. Aboveground tissue concentrations were measured after acid digestion and ICP-OES (Section 2.7, PDF p. 8); BCF was calculated for Cd, Pb, and Zn (Section 2.7, PDF p. 8; BCF results Section 3.3, PDF pp. 19–21).

## Contamination origin and soil source

This is **naturally contaminated, field-derived soil**, not an artificial dosing study. On **18 June 2021**, the authors manually collected seven 18.9-L buckets of **soil and chat-pile-derived-sediment mixtures in unknown proportions** from approximately the top 10–15 cm at three locations within an approximately 22-ha decommissioned chat-processing area associated with the **Tar Creek Superfund site near Picher, Oklahoma** (Section 2.1, PDF p. 4). The three source environments were explicitly described as follows (Section 2.1, PDF pp. 4–5): low contamination from the bed of a dry stormwater retention pond; medium contamination from stormwater-induced alluvium immediately adjacent to a chat pile; and high contamination from a stream bank near Tar Creek, close to a human-made diversion channel.

The article gives field-XRF estimates at collection of approximately 550–600 mg Pb kg−1, <1000 mg Zn kg−1, and <20 mg Cd kg−1 for low; approximately 1500–1800 mg Pb kg−1, approximately 2000 mg Zn kg−1, and approximately 60 mg Cd kg−1 for medium; and approximately 5500 mg Pb kg−1, approximately 13,000 mg Zn kg−1, and approximately 123 mg Cd kg−1 for high (Section 2.1, PDF p. 4). The final laboratory characterization is in **Table 1, PDF p. 5**. For the BCF denominator used by the paper, initial **water-soluble Cd** concentrations in Table 1 are: low **0.2 mg kg−1**, medium **1.3 mg kg−1**, and high **1.1 mg kg−1**. Table 1 also reports pH 6.27, 6.23, and 6.53 and soil organic matter 3.1%, 2.1%, and 4.6% for low, medium, and high, respectively; these are included here as study descriptors, not imputed values.

No experimental Cd/Pb/Zn spiking, clean-soil dilution, chelation, hydroponics, or nutrient solution was used to establish contamination. The study did apply standard N/P/K fertilizer to all pot mixtures (Section 2.4, PDF pp. 6–7), but fertilizer was not a metal-spiking or exposure-changing treatment and was common experimental management. Biochar itself is a treatment factor and is handled separately below.

## Field versus greenhouse design and exposure duration

The study is a **greenhouse pot experiment using real Superfund-site soil**, not a field planting experiment. It used three soil contamination levels, four biochar rates (0, 2, 5, and 10% v/v), and two cultivars in a complete factorial design with **four replications per soil–biochar–cultivar combination**, for **96 experimental units/pots**, organized as a completely random design (Section 2.2, PDF p. 6). Biochar was mixed into the top 15 cm of soil; 0, 2, 5, and 10% v/v corresponded to approximately 0, 0.5, 1.25, and 2.5% by mass (Section 2.2, PDF p. 6). The biochar was Douglas-fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) derived, in 3–5 mm flakes (Section 2.3, PDF p. 6).

All plants were exposed to the soil–biochar–fertilizer mixture for **90 consecutive days** before destructive harvest, although the 90-day periods did not begin simultaneously because of poor initial germination and later transplanting (Sections 2.5 and 2.7, PDF pp. 7–8). Aboveground and belowground tissue were harvested separately; roots were washed, and aboveground tissue was dried at 65 °C for 48 h before ICP-OES analysis (Section 2.7, PDF p. 8).

## Amendment and eligibility handling

The study’s biochar treatment is explicitly a remediation/exposure manipulation. Therefore:

* **0% biochar** is the eligible unamended control subset and is retained.
* **2%, 5%, and 10% biochar** are excluded from the locked primary target observations because biochar is an exposure-changing amendment.
* The common fertilizer application is not treated as a primary exposure-changing amendment because it supplied ordinary plant nutrients equally according to soil treatment and was not used to dose or chelate Cd, Pb, or Zn.

The 0% arm remains a valid naturally contaminated soil observation set because the soil was collected from Tar Creek and not made contaminated by experimental dosing or dilution.

## BCF definition and compatibility

The paper states in **Section 2.7, “Plant Measurements and Sample Collection, Processing, and Analyses,” PDF p. 8**:

> “The bioconcentration factor (BCF) was used for the assessment of the metal concentration in the aboveground tissue relative to the initial soil concentration.”

It then specifies that BCF was calculated, pot by pot, by dividing **aboveground Cd, Zn, and Pb tissue concentrations (mg kg−1)** by the **mean initial water-soluble Cd, Zn, and Pb concentrations (mg kg−1)** for the corresponding soil. The authors explicitly note that this denominator is a deviation from Yoon et al., who used total recoverable soil concentration, because the authors considered water-soluble concentration a better indicator of environmentally available metal. Thus the extracted BCF definition is:

**BCF = aboveground plant-tissue metal concentration (mg kg−1) / mean initial water-soluble soil metal concentration (mg kg−1).**

The ratio is dimensionless. Both numerator and denominator are reported on a dry-mass concentration basis in the paper’s analytical workflow; no fresh-weight conversion is needed for the extracted Table 5 values. This is a compatible plant/soil concentration ratio for the target dataset, with the denominator basis explicitly retained in each observation note.

### Pb and Zn observations deliberately not extracted

The article reports Pb and Zn BCFs in text and figures, but not with the exact 0%-biochar-by-cultivar treatment cells needed here. **Figure 4 (PDF pp. 19–20)** plots BCF by soil–cultivar combination averaged across biochar rates; the text reports examples such as Pb BCF 207 for high/Carmagnola and 45.9 for low/Carmagnola, and Zn BCF 47.6 for high/Carmagnola and 6.7 for medium/Jinma (Section 3.3, PDF pp. 19–20). **Figure 5 (PDF p. 21)** reports Pb BCF by soil–biochar rate averaged across cultivars, including high/10% BC 487 and low/0% BC 36.7. These are not exact cultivar-specific 0% control rows. Because the locked protocol says not to infer or digitize when exact directly reported values are unavailable, no Pb or Zn rows are included.

## Independence, duplicate, and shared-experiment checks

### Independence from Ha et al. 2025

The supplied `final_overlap_audit_report.json` lists seven confirmed DOI overlaps and **does not include 10.3390/soilsystems8040114**; it also reports zero probable-overlap flags. The candidate is a 2024 University of Arkansas study using Tar Creek Superfund soil, whereas the Ha et al. 2025 study is a distinct later publication in the supplied external-candidate context. The inspected B1 authors, field site, sample-collection date (18 June 2021), and greenhouse design do not match a Ha et al. study record. There is no evidence in the supplied audit or inspected primary source that the B1 observations are a reanalysis of Ha et al.’s experimental units. **Assessment: independent from Ha = likely yes.**

### Relationship to Thurston’s 2023 thesis

The University of Arkansas repository identifies the 2023 thesis **“Evaluation of Biochar Rate and Hemp Cultivar for the Phytoremediation of Heavy-Metal-Contaminated Soil from the Tar Creek Superfund Site”** (Dietrich Thurston, MS thesis, graduation August 2023; https://scholarworks.uark.edu/etd/4868/). Its abstract independently reports the same Tar Creek greenhouse study structure: *Cannabis sativa* cultivars ‘Carmagnola’ and ‘Jinma’, biochar rates 0/2/5/10% v/v, low/medium/high contamination levels, and 90 days in contaminated Tar Creek soil. The 2024 article has the same lead author, advisor/research group, site, treatment levels, and design. Therefore the journal article and thesis are **shared/duplicate reporting of the same experimental campaign**, not independent experiments. The present B1 report treats the 2024 peer-reviewed article as the primary source for the candidate and does not count the thesis as another independent study.

### Relationship to B2 and B5 candidates

B2 is a *Phyllostachys praecox* bamboo monoculture/intercropping candidate in a separate bamboo/Sedum research line and site; no shared authorship, Tar Creek soil, sample date, or experimental units were found. B5’s candidate title “Selenium Metabolism in Hemp” resolves in the primary literature to Stonehouse et al. 2020, *Environmental Science & Technology* 54:4221–4230, DOI 10.1021/acs.est.9b07747, with different authors and a different study focus. The B5 paper is cited in B1’s reference list as an external comparison; the B1 methods do not cite or reuse B5 experimental units. **Assessment: no evidence of shared experiment with B2 or B5; B1 is independent from those candidates.**

## Primary-source limitations and caveats

The article’s Data Availability Statement says: “Data presented in this article are available upon request” (PDF p. 30). No supplementary numeric data file was supplied on the inspected article page. Consequently, only the exact values printed in Table 5 are extracted. Figure-only Pb/Zn values are intentionally not converted into observations. The 2024 article’s eligible contribution is therefore a **subset** of its full design: unamended 0% biochar controls from naturally contaminated Tar Creek soil.
