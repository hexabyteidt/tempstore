## Species/taxa and elements

The exact taxon is **castor, *Ricinus communis* L.** The main text names this species in the introduction and methods; the methods list 15 varieties: ZM3, ZM5, CS24, ZBM3, JZM4, FM10, YM6, YM8, TM7, JM6, TGM302, DM2, DM5, TM16, and TM24 (PDF pp. 3–4, Section 2.2). Supplementary Table S1 expands those processing codes to variety names. The target elements are **Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn**.

## Contamination origin and soil system

The study area is a lead–zinc mining area in eastern Yunnan, China, at 26°52′04″–26°52′15″ N and 103°59′44″–104°00′40″ E (PDF p. 3, Section 2.1; Figure 1). The paper states that decades of mining, beneficiation, smelting, and resource-recycling activity caused severe contamination of surrounding farmland soils. It explicitly links the spatial decline in soil metal concentration to distance from the mining area and concludes that mining activities are a major source of soil accumulation (PDF pp. 7–8, Section 3.1). This is naturally contaminated, field-derived soil; there is no indication that the study soil was artificially dosed with Cd, Pb, Cu, or Zn.

Soil was sampled on **27 March 2024** from five points per experimental plot using 0–50 cm soil columns, separated into five depth intervals and composited by depth (PDF pp. 3–4, Section 2.2). The reported study-area soil concentrations in **Table 1 (PDF p. 8)** are:

| Element | Mean (mg kg−1) | Range (mg kg−1) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Pb | 216 | 57–500 |
| Cd | 19.80 | 1.91–52.34 |
| Cu | 165 | 44.45–259 |
| Zn | 969 | 146–4126 |

Table 1 also reports pH mean 5.99, range 5.58–6.85; the “actual castor planting zone” properties in Section 2.1 give pH 6.31 and organic matter 31.2 g kg−1. The article does not provide a numerical, variety-linked rhizosphere-soil concentration table; Supplementary Figure S2 is graphical only.

## Field/greenhouse design, amendments, and exposure duration

This was a **field experiment**, not hydroponic, nutrient-solution, or greenhouse-only exposure. The experiment ran from **28 April to 21 October 2024** (approximately 176 days; PDF p. 4, Section 2.2). It used a randomized complete block design with **45 plots**, 15 varieties, and **three replicates per variety**. Each plot was 1 m × 9 m, with 100 cm × 90 cm spacing and a planting density of 25,012 plants ha−1 (PDF p. 4, Section 2.2).

The authors applied compound fertilizer at 450 kg ha−1 as basal fertilizer and urea at 225 kg ha−1 as top dressing at flowering (PDF p. 4, Section 2.2). These are standard agronomic fertilization inputs described as field management, not a metal-spiking, dilution, chelation, or primarily exposure-changing amendment. No artificial Cd/Pb/Cu/Zn dosing, contaminated-substrate mixing, clean-soil dilution, chelator, biochar, sludge, phosphogypsum, or hydroponic medium is reported. Analytical “spiked recovery tests” in Section 2.3 refer to laboratory quality assurance, not experimental soil spiking, and must not be interpreted as an exposure treatment.

## BCF definition and what was actually numerically reported

The paper explicitly defines BCF in **Section 2.4.2, “Comprehensive Bioconcentration Index,” PDF p. 6, Equation (1)**:

> BCFxᵢ = Cxᵢ / Csoil

The paper states that **Cxᵢ** is the heavy-metal concentration in castor roots, stems, leaves, or grains, in mg kg−1, and **Csoil** is the heavy-metal concentration in soil, also in mg kg−1. Thus the definition is compatible in form with a plant-organ dry-weight concentration divided by soil concentration, with a dimensionless ratio. The paper does not explicitly state fresh versus dry weight in the BCF equation, although the methods state that plant samples were dried before analysis (PDF p. 4, Section 2.2).

The paper then defines a **CBCI**, not a BCF, as the mean of four membership-function-transformed BCFs: CBCI = 1/4 Σu(xᵢ), Equation (2), PDF p. 6. Numerical CBCI ranges and selected values are reported in **Section 3.4, PDF p. 10**, and Figure 5. These CBCI values cannot be relabeled as element-specific BCF observations.

The paper’s numerical result presentation is insufficient for locked extraction:

* **Figure 4 (PDF p. 10)** plots Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn concentrations in roots, stems, leaves, and seeds, with axes in mg kg−1. It does not provide a numeric table of the plotted values, and the figure is not a numeric BCF table.
* **Section 3.3 (PDF pp. 9–10)** gives only selected concentration examples, including TM7 root Pb 25.21 mg kg−1, TGM302 grain Cu 29.25 mg kg−1, CS24 grain Zn 68.72 mg kg−1, and TM16 stem Zn 536 mg kg−1. These isolated plant values do not provide the corresponding variety-specific soil denominator needed for an exact BCF.
* **Figure 5 and Section 3.4 (PDF p. 10)** report CBCI values/ranges, e.g., root CBCI 0.26–0.87, stem 0.09–0.92, leaf 0.21–0.78, and grain 0.22–0.62, including TM16 stem CBCI 0.92 and TGM302 grain CBCI 0.69. CBCI is a transformed multi-metal index and is not an eligible BCF row.
* **Supplementary Figure S2, supplementary PDF p. 5**, plots rhizosphere-soil Cd, Cu, Pb, and Zn concentrations by variety but supplies no numerical table. Supplementary Table S1 is a variety-name key; Table S2 reports plant height and root length; Supplementary Figure S1 reports dry weights. None supplies exact paired plant/soil concentrations for BCF reconstruction.

Therefore, no BCF value is placed in the observations array. Digitizing Figure 4 or Supplementary Figure S2 would create approximate graphical estimates, not values directly reported in a table/text, and would not satisfy the locked requirement that BCF be directly reported or calculable from directly reported compatible concentrations. The isolated plant concentration examples likewise cannot be safely divided by a matching variety-specific soil concentration.
